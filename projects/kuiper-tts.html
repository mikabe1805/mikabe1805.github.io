Two readings of the same equation

Screen readers handle math poorly, and a literal reading like “d q over d t” is hard to follow by ear. In January 2026 I rendered all 46 equations of a 1971 antenna paper twice: once as the literal narration, and once rewritten the way a person would say it, with derivatives named, ratios spelled out and pauses at chained equalities. Those rules became the paper’s structure-aware equation verbalization.

Play either reading and the equation lights up in step with the voice. Point at or tap any part of the equation to see how each reading says it, or move through its “Hear both” buttons.

What the rewrite changed

  1. Derivative named

    d q over d t→the derivative of q with respect to t,

  2. A pause at the equals sign

    d t equals C times→to t, equals C times

  3. Grouping spoken

    sine of 2 pi f t→sine of, open parenthesis, two pi f t, close parenthesis

How an equation becomes speech

The first two stages are the team’s pipeline, and the last two are my work. Below them, one equation from the 1971 paper is traced through all four.

  1. 01Team

    Page to LaTeX

    The team’s document-conversion pipeline renders each PDF page and recovers LaTeX with a vision-language model.

  2. 02Team

    Narration in the source

    The same pipeline writes a first spoken narration of each equation into the LaTeX.

  3. 03My work

    Narration for the ear

    My rules rewrite that narration: derivatives named, ratios spelled out, pauses at chained equalities, and a speaking rate that slows for complex equations.

  4. 04My work

    Speech

    My tool hands the result to Piper, an open-source offline speech engine, with a slower math voice preset.

A voice trained on my own recordings

I designed a coverage-balanced recording corpus and a pronunciation testbench, then recorded 886 clips of my own voice, about 41 minutes, with a terminal recording tool I built. A one-command pipeline on the lab’s Jetstream2 allocation trims silence, normalizes loudness, rejects clipped or noisy takes, resumes from checkpoints and exports the trained voice to ONNX.

I turned the pipeline into a step-by-step guide; another student recorded 341 clips with it, and I trained a second voice from them.

Finding the pitch jumps

Symptom
One version of my voice developed audible pitch jumps.
Cause
An augmentation step made faster and slower copies of each clip by resampling, which also shifted their pitch about a semitone up or down.
Fix
I replaced it with time-stretching, which changes speed and keeps pitch, and retrained.
Result
Version 5 is the voice after the fix.

Checking a caching idea the way a listener hears it

At Prof. Kruger’s request, before the repository existed, I tested whether Piper audio could be cached word by word and reused. The first analysis compared raw waveforms and found none of nine test contexts cacheable. I argued that what matters is what a listener hears; a re-analysis with a perceptual spectral measure found all nine cacheable, and the findings document records the correction.
