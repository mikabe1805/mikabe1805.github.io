Browser breadboard for Rutgers ECE labs

Knowsy Breadboard Lab

A virtual breadboard for Rutgers’ Digital Logic Design and Principles of EE II labs. Students can wire switches, LEDs, passives and 74LS chips with their datasheet pinouts the way they would at the bench, and a schematic draws itself from the wiring. I started the module in May 2026 and led its three-person team.

Wired like the bench

As a student wires the board, the lab draws a schematic from the wiring. Each gate gets its symbol from the chip’s pinout, unused gates are left out, and hovering a net on the board or in the schematic lights it in both.

Board features

  • Rails and the center trench

    + and − rails run along both edges, and DIP chips straddle a center trench between five-hole tie strips, as on the boards at the bench.

  • Datasheet pinouts

    Chips use their datasheet pinouts. Hovering a pin shows its name, number, role and live level.

  • Wires that stay clear

    Jumpers route through the board’s empty channels, so they never cover a hole or a chip. Students can bend them by hand, and a bend never changes the circuit.

  • Boards sized like the lab’s

    30, 52 or 63 columns wide, based on the BB400 and BB830 boards, with up to six boards stacked, each with its own rails.

How the lab reads a net

Every net resolves to one of four levels, and drivers have strength. A pull-up drives weakly, so a closed switch wins without a false conflict, while two strong drivers that disagree are a real one. This sketch applies the lab engine’s rule to the Lab 1 circuit.

The probe and meter show the lab’s logic-level readings: 5.00 V for high, 0.00 V for low, 1.60 V for a floating net and 2.50 V for a conflict. The Lab 6 manual teaches students to read about 1.6 V as an input with no driver.

Why? The lab’s own card for the pull-up step reads “Pull-up is the weak default; the switch is the strong override.”

The analog side

For the EE II labs I built a modified-nodal-analysis solver over complex admittances. It covers resistors, capacitors, inductors and an ideal op-amp, solves with dense complex Gaussian elimination and partial pivoting, and drives the oscilloscope and the Bode plotter.

The EE II guided lab mixes 12 Morse-keyed tones into one input signal. A student tunes a bandpass filter onto one channel, with a Q of at least 20 and every other channel 20 dB down, and the lab decodes that channel’s three-letter word. Guided labs

Eight guided labs based on the course materials, 32 sections and 90 steps, check themselves by electrical connection, so wire color can’t fail a step. Steps that hinge on a real concept carry a short “Why?” card, 35 in all. The guided steps and their cards were my idea, and I brought them to the PI.

Three of the lab’s 35 “Why?” cards, quoted as written

Why?Lab 4

Subtraction in the adder

“Subtracting is just inverting and adding one.”

“Feed the adder Y flipped bit-for-bit, then sneak a +1 in through the carry input. Invert-plus-one is exactly −Y in two's complement, so X − Y falls out of a plain adder with no subtract circuit.”

Why?Lab 5

D flip-flops and the clock edge

“A flip-flop only peeks at D on the clock's rising edge.”

“The S-R latch follows its inputs every instant; a D flip-flop samples D only when the clock ticks up and ignores it the rest of the time. That edge discipline is what makes synchronous systems predictable.”

Why?Lab 6

Decoupling the flip-flop

“A decoupling cap is a tiny battery that catches each switching spike.”

“When the flip-flops flip, they yank a fast current spike off the supply, and the jumper back to the rail lets the local voltage sag. A 0.1 µF cap across the power pins supplies that spike so the chip's power never dips and glitches your state.”

The pre-release audit

I directed an audit before the planned release and chose what to fix. The five findings below shipped fixed in the release PR; smaller items, such as the Bode panel’s Q sampling, went on a follow-up list.

Decisions I made for the release PR

  • Fix the pinouts to the real datasheets.
  • Export Verilog with a testbench for the student’s own Icarus run, and keep a WebAssembly simulator out of the page.
  • Add tri-state parts and a bus lab.

  1. Lab 4 passed on an unwired board

    The reference build had +5 V and ground merged into one net, and a fallback calculation hid it. The fallback was removed, so results now come from the simulated board.

  2. Flip-flops ignored their clock pin

    They now clock from the 555 wired to their CLK pin, so the Lab 6 traffic controller steps through its states.

  3. Four pinouts didn’t match the TI datasheets

    The 74LS151, 74LS153, 74LS283 and 74LS20. The 74LS283 had ground on pin 15; the datasheet puts it on pin 8.

  4. Wiring on stacked boards was dead

    Wires on a second or later board now join the circuit.

  5. A cross-site scripting hole in share links

    A wire color in a crafted share link reached the board’s SVG unescaped. The fix closed it.

My part and the team’s

I built the breadboard UX, the schematic renderer, the debugging tools, the circuit solver and most of the logic engine. A teammate built the sequential simulator (latches, flip-flops and the 555) and early Lab 4 work. Builds export to Verilog with a testbench.
