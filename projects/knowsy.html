Learn a circuit by working with it.

A breadboard is easier to understand when students can build a circuit and observe what changes. Knowsy’s breadboard lab brings component placement, wiring, and simulation into a browser-based learning environment.

My responsibility is the breadboard module, working with Professor Kruger and managing a three-person team including me.

This example calculates a simple voltage divider in the browser. The breadboard lab supports a broader set of circuits through its own simulation engine.

Make the simulation explainable.

The digital simulation represents HIGH, LOW, FLOAT, and CONFLICT explicitly, alongside behavior for 74LS-series components. Keeping these states separate helps distinguish an undriven signal from an actual logical low or competing drivers.

The module also includes an analog solver using Modified Nodal Analysis over complex admittances, plus Bode analysis for frequency response. This is analytical software work where the expected behavior can be checked against known circuit formulas.

Compare against something independent.

Tests cover known circuits such as dividers and filters, as well as frequency-response calculations. Formula-based expectations help prevent a visually plausible simulation from quietly teaching the wrong behavior.

The next step in evaluating a learning tool is observing students use it: where the explanation helps, where they get stuck, and whether the feedback supports their understanding.

Software fundamentals travel well.

Explicit state, inspectable behavior, and independent checks matter in AI systems too. This project is part of the software foundation I bring to that work, along with experience owning a module and coordinating a small team.
